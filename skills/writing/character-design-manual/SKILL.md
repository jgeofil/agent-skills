# Character Design Manual

A structured operational manual for designing high-retention fiction characters, orchestrating ensemble dynamics, and reverse-engineering character architectures from existing works.

## When to Use

- Designing a protagonist, supporting character, or antagonist from scratch.
- Auditing or fixing a character that feels flat, unlikable, passive, or overpowered without tension.
- Structuring character motivations, internal conflicts, and multi-layer persona contrasts.
- Mapping ensemble relationship networks, workplace/faction hierarchies, and character pacing across chapters.
- Disassembling (reverse-engineering) an existing character from source material into reusable structural patterns before drafting a new original character.

## Steps

### Step 1: Route the Task and Select Mode

Determine whether you are operating in **Creation Mode** (building or refining a character) or **Disassembly Mode** (deconstructing an existing character from a text).

1. **Creation Mode**: Select the target module from the routing table below, fill out the required structural template, apply the behavioral rules, and validate against the Quality Checklist in Step 8.
2. **Disassembly Mode**: Extract the source character's attributes against the corresponding template item by item, identify the underlying structural mechanics (contrast layers, function, motivation chain, and recurring hooks), and abstract those mechanics into a clean blueprint so new characters replicate the structural tension without copying surface tropes.

| Design Target | Primary Frameworks | Reference Step |
|---|---|---|
| Protagonist | Protagonist Card, Dual Identity/Cheat Matrix, Prestige & Red-Line Rules | Step 2 |
| Supporting Cast & Ensemble | Supporting Card, White-Glove Archetype, Character Pool Weaving, Hub Deduction | Step 3 |
| Antagonist / Villain | 4-Tier Villain Hierarchy, 4-Element Setup, Mirror-Flaw Construction | Step 4 |
| Motivation & Conflict | 4-Link Motivation Chain, 4 Internal Conflict Archetypes | Step 5 |
| Depth, Contrast & Arcs | 3-Layer Label Contrast, 6 Contrast Dimensions, 9-Dimension Framework, 3-Stage Arc | Step 6 |
| Relationships & Pacing | Strong/Medium/Weak Ties, 4 Intimacy Stages, Workplace Matrix, 5-Chapter Cyclic Loop | Step 7 |

### Step 2: Architect the Protagonist

Use this template and rule set when building or fixing a protagonist.

```text
Name:
Gender / Age:
Role Positioning: [One sentence defining the protagonist's core dramatic function]
Explicit Identity: [Visible social role/status driving early-story conflict]
Implicit Identity: [Hidden lineage, past, or secret driving mid-to-late-story conflict]
Explicit Cheat / Edge: [External system, artifact, foresight, or resource active from Chapter 1]
Implicit Cheat / Edge: [Core personality trait or cognitive edge that makes the character irreplaceable]
Physical Anchors: [3-5 distinct visual keywords, e.g., tall and lean, faded leather jacket, left-hand burn scar, half-awake eyes]
Personality Keywords: [3-5 traits containing at least one internal contradiction, e.g., sharp-tongued but fiercely protective, outwardly lazy but calculating]
Core Goal: [Concrete end-of-book objective]
Core Motivation: [Emotional driver behind the goal - never abstract ambition]
Fatal Flaw: [Character-driven psychological blind spot that causes costly mistakes at key plot turns, not an ability deficit]
Signature Hook: [Recurring catchphrase, habit, or micro-action for instant reader recognition]
```

#### Protagonist Design Rules

1. **Ground motivation in emotion**: Concrete emotional stakes (e.g., avenging a murdered parent, protecting a sibling's freedom) outperform abstract goals (e.g., becoming the strongest).
2. **Establish character through early high-stakes choices**: Force a defining moral or tactical decision in the opening chapters. Intellectual victories and outsmarting superior foes establish competence faster than brute force.
3. **Maintain protagonist prestige**: Readers require competence and dignity to stay immersed. Avoid whiny internal monologues, self-pity, or letting minor characters humiliate the protagonist unchecked.
4. **Unify world tone and persona**: Align social identity, backstory, special advantage (cheat/golden finger), and personality with the genre's emotional expectations. Use the rules of the protagonist's special advantage to incentivize and rationalize their signature behavior so supporting characters' reactions naturally highlight the protagonist.
5. **Manage power scaling and expectation ceilings**: When the protagonist's strength or status nears a ceiling and tension drops (steamrolling weak opponents without stakes), suppress expectations without nerfing competence:
   - Introduce cross-dimensional gaps (dominant in combat, vulnerable in politics or logistics).
   - Deliver unexpected compounding gains after calculated risks.
   - Ensure the protagonist derives tangible rewards from displaying power rather than endlessly sacrificing without return.
6. **Compensate for aloof/one-dimensional protagonists with a companion matrix**: If the protagonist is intentionally stoic or single-minded to maximize immersion and minimize unforced errors, pair them with 3-5 companions (or non-human/pet sidekicks) distributed across four roles:
   - *Comedic Foil*: Absorbs awkwardness and minor setbacks that would damage the protagonist's prestige.
   - *Mouthpiece*: Voices boasts, taunts, or exposition the protagonist is too reserved to say aloud.
   - *Contrast/Deadpan*: Creates humor through earnestness or clashing temperament.
   - *Utility Specialist*: Covers specific tactical blind spots despite timidity or quirks.

### Step 3: Engineer Supporting Characters and Ensemble Networks

Supporting characters exist to execute plot functions and refract the protagonist's impact. Never introduce a character without a clear dramatic job.

```text
Name:
Character Function: [Mentor / Ally / Intelligence Source / Sacrificial Catalyst / Foil / Expectation Hub]
Relationship to Protagonist: [Hierarchical and emotional link]
Core Labels: [1-2 prominent traits or tropes]
Contrast Dimension: [Selected from the 6 Contrast Dimensions in Step 6]
Signature Anchor: [One unforgettable visual, verbal, or behavioral quirk]
Planned Exit / Transition: [How and when the character leaves the active stage or shifts role]
```

#### Supporting Cast Rules

1. **Cap active scene density**: Limit speaking supporting characters to at most 3 per scene to prevent dialogue clutter and reader confusion.
2. **Calibrate dimensionality by role**: Functionality precedes three-dimensionality. High-tier mysteries or apex mentors often lose their aura of dread or authority when over-explained ("over-humanizing weakens mystique; ruthless ambiguity amplifies threat"). Reserve full 3D depth for recurring core cast.
3. **Deploy the "White-Glove" composite archetype**: When the protagonist must maintain a clean or aloof image, assign a trusted lieutenant to handle five functions:
   - Speak truths or boasts the protagonist cannot voice directly.
   - Execute ruthless measures on the protagonist's behalf.
   - Defend the protagonist's reputation immediately against slander.
   - Expose and broadcast the antagonist's schemes to win public opinion.
   - Provide timely humor and validation to regulate scene tension.
4. **Use Supporting Characters as Expectation Hubs**: Designate a core supporting character as a "mission base" who holds multiple short- and long-term expectations. Have the protagonist return to this hub after resolving an arc to trigger reaction payoffs and launch the next plot unit. If the hub character exits, replace the loss immediately with an unexpected windfall to offset reader loss aversion.
5. **Weave the Character Pool ("Character-to-Character" Introduction)**: Introduce new cast members through established, well-liked characters (e.g., a popular ally's rival, mentor, or sibling) so reader investment transfers automatically.
6. **Control appearance pacing**: Rotate popular supporting characters in every 3-5 chapters rather than every chapter to preserve novelty. Every appearance must deliver new information or advance a plot function. Drop characters cleanly when their narrative utility ends.
7. **Apply the Obsession Method for episodic side characters**: Anchor minor arc characters around a single extreme obsession that governs their speech and choices, tempered with one relatable human detail so they remain believable.

### Step 4: Construct Antagonists by Tier

Match the villain's design complexity to their narrative lifespan. A weak or illogical villain devalues the protagonist's triumph.

#### 4.1 Villain Hierarchy Table

| Tier | Span | Dramatic Function | Design Requirements | Resolution / Exit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minor Villain | 1-5 chapters | Immediate tactical obstacle | 1-2 sharp negative labels (arrogant, greedy, predatory) | Swift, decisive defeat or public exposure |
| Mid-Boss | 10-30 chapters | Volume antagonist | Concrete personal motive (never "evil for evil's sake"); distinct resources/schemes; must defeat or corner the protagonist at least once | Direct, high-catharsis showdown |
| Major Boss | 1-3 volumes | Phase-defining ideological conflict | Full character arc, coherent worldview, clashes with protagonist's philosophy, forces protagonist into a desperate crisis, possesses one admirable or sympathetic quality | Ritualistic climax with lingering narrative aftermath |
| Final Boss | Full work | Embodiment of core thematic opposition | Foreshadowed from Chapter 1; overwhelming power/influence; requires the protagonist to undergo fundamental transformation to overcome | Thematic and existential resolution |

#### 4.2 Four Sequential Elements of Villain Setup

Execute all four steps in order when introducing a mid-tier or higher antagonist:

1. **Demonstrate capability immediately**: Show lethal competence, intelligence, or resource dominance in their first scene.
2. **Establish internal logic**: Make their goal completely rational from their own worldview and upbringing.
3. **Deliver a genuine threat**: Allow them to score a real victory or inflict a painful cost on the protagonist's camp.
4. **Conceal ultimate intent**: Hold back their deepest motive for a mid-arc twist that reframes earlier actions.

#### 4.3 Four-Step Mirror-Flaw Architecture

1. **Self-protagonism**: Write the villain as the hero of their own narrative. Give recurring antagonists their own supporting foils and growth across map transitions rather than discarding them for generic stronger replacements.
2. **Traumatic root**: Define who shaped them, what they lost, and what pain they refuse to feel again.
3. **Weaponized flaw**: Under adversity, have the villain double down on their dark traits until their greatest strength becomes their fatal blind spot.
4. **Protagonist mirror**: Construct the villain's strengths to target the protagonist's exact weaknesses so every collision generates natural friction.
5. **Objective collision over arbitrary targeting ("Dominate Without Dominating")**: Do not make antagonists target the protagonist out of nowhere. Give the antagonist a macro-objective that organically crushes the protagonist's group or interests, forcing an unavoidable clash driven by incompatible worldviews.

### Step 5: Build the Motivation and Conflict Chain

Use this chain whenever a character's actions feel arbitrary or when plot momentum stalls.

```text
Trigger (Cause): [Specific, visceral inciting incident - e.g., "publicly framed and stripped of rank", not "treated unfairly"]
Intent (Goal): [Immediate and long-term action the character chooses]
Constraint (Obstacle): [Structural power gap, rule, or resource limit preventing an easy win]
Stakes (Risk): [Concrete, irreversible loss if the character fails AND the personal cost of succeeding]
```

#### Internal Conflict Selection Matrix

When engineering a character crisis or pivotal decision scene, pit two valid drives against each other:

| Conflict Type | Core Tension | Operational Example |
|---|---|---|
| Goal vs. Morality | Desired outcome vs. ethical boundary | Achieving vengeance requires sacrificing an innocent bystander |
| Emotion vs. Interest | Personal attachment vs. rational survival | Protecting a compromised ally at the cost of strategic exposure |
| Short-Term vs. Long-Term | Immediate relief vs. ultimate ambition | Accepting a humiliating truce now to survive for future reckoning |
| Self vs. Collective | Individual freedom vs. duty/expectations | Wanting a quiet life while bound to a collapsing family or faction |

### Step 6: Apply Contrast, Depth, and Arc Mechanics

Character design is functional soul engineering, not a static demographic sheet of height, weight, and hobbies.

#### 6.1 Three-Layer Label Contrast Method

Assign three layers to every major character. Layer 1 and Layer 2 may align, but Layer 3 must invert expectations.

| Layer | Definition | Example Profile | Behavioral Demonstration (Show, Don't Tell) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Identity Label | Public social role or stereotype | Disgraced heiress cast out of her family | — |
| 2. Performance Label | Outward persona shown to acquaintances | Meek, submissive, non-confrontational | Lowers head when insulted; leaves quietly when evicted |
| 3. Core Label | True inner nature and value orientation | Cold, patient strategist | Secretly records every conversation, plants audit traps, liquidates family assets in advance |

#### 6.2 Six Dimensions of Quick Contrast

For rapid supporting character creation, pick 1-2 pairs:

- **Surface Persona vs. Inner Nature**: Smiling diplomat who holds ruthless grudges; sharp-tongued cynic who secretly funds orphans.
- **Appearance vs. Surface Persona**: Scarred giant who speaks with timid politeness.
- **Appearance vs. Inner Nature**: Frail, angelic scholar who orchestrates merciless purges.
- **Identity vs. Lifestyle**: Billionaire heir living in a cramped basement eating instant noodles.
- **Identity vs. Skill/Hobby**: Syndicate assassin obsessed with delicate pastry baking.
- **Identity vs. Inner Ideal**: Hardened mercenary with a romantic, poetic soul.

#### 6.3 Behavioral Consistency and Show-Don't-Tell Techniques

1. **Reveal traits through sensitivity and avoidance**: Show how a character reacts when their sore spot is touched (panic, deflection, cold silence), and pay attention to what they *refuse* to do, which reveals deeper values than routine actions.
2. **Establish appeal before flaws**: Showcase competence, wit, or warmth first so the audience invests before exposing character flaws.
3. **Repeat signature behaviors across varied contexts**: Translate a core trait into a concrete behavioral loop or catchphrase/meme and trigger it across different scenes to lock in reader memory.
4. **Never bend persona for plot convenience**: If a planned plot twist requires a character to act out of character, rewrite the plot setup rather than breaking the character's logic.
5. **Keep mistakes within forgivable bounds**: Readers forgive setbacks caused by information asymmetry or overwhelming force majeure; they abandon stories when protagonists fail due to sudden stupidity, naive mercy toward lethal enemies, or blunders far below their established tier.
6. **Demonstrate high EQ/intelligence through scene turns**: Never state that a character is clever. Stage a conversational or tactical trap where the reader expects an awkward or fatal outcome, then have the character read micro-expressions, reframe the narrative, and reverse the power dynamic.

#### 6.4 Nine-Dimensional Framework & Three-Stage Growth Arc

For full-book characters, map across nine dimensions: **Basic Profile (Past/Present)**, **Contrasting Persona**, **Signature Habits/Vices**, **High-Stakes Relationship Network**, **Origin/Upbringing**, **Growth Arc**, **Thematic Foil/Reflection**, **Status-Driven Values**, and **Defining Biographical Episodes**.

Structure long-term psychological evolution across three stages:

1. **Ego (Mask / Early Stage)**: Driven by external validation and survival; trying to become who others expect or fear (generates *Identification*).
2. **Self (Friction / Mid Stage)**: Suffering the collision between the outward mask and authentic internal desires (generates *Sympathy & Resonance*).
3. **Integrated Self (Mastery / Late Stage)**: Aligning true internal values with outward action regardless of external judgment (generates *Deep Empathy*).

### Step 7: Orchestrate Relationships, Pacing, and Immersion

#### 7.1 Relationship Layering (Strong / Medium / Weak Ties)

- **Strong Ties (Minimum 3 per protagonist)**: Attributes and bonds that directly drive main-plot conflict and core payoffs (primary power source, faction status, blood feud, central rival/partner). Never let minor quirks overshadow strong ties.
- **Medium Ties**: Functional bonds belonging to supporting characters that generate subplot momentum without eclipsing the protagonist.
- **Weak Ties**: Flavor preferences and minor habits (e.g., love for sweets, fear of heights) used strictly to texture scenes and humanize the cast.

#### 7.2 Four Stages of Relationship Progression & Intimacy Regression

Track how dialogue and behavior shift as two characters move closer:

1. **Encounter (Stranger)**: Guarded, polite distance, surface observation.
2. **Acquaintance (Associate)**: Testing boundaries, reading expressions, tactical flattery or banter.
3. **Understanding (Confidant)**: Knowing boundaries, candid teasing, sharing vulnerabilities.
4. **Intimacy (Core Bond)**: Exposing inner scars, possessiveness, unspoken coordination.
   - *Intimacy Regression Rule*: Once a character enters Stage 4, they can no longer effortlessly perform their Stage 1-2 defense mechanisms. For example, a bold, teasing character who effortlessly flirts with strangers becomes hesitant, flustered, or over-cautious when confronting genuine intimacy with the protagonist. Keeping early detachment in the intimate stage breaks emotional credibility.

#### 7.3 Workplace / Faction Hierarchy Model

Treat every story setting (sect, academy, corporation, military unit, syndicate) as a workplace ecosystem:

1. Define the **Hierarchy** (superiors, peers, subordinates), **Cooperation Modes** (resource trading, shared missions), and **Competition Vectors** (promotion bottlenecks, scarce resources).
2. Generate organic friction from status gaps and resource scarcity.
3. Once the protagonist achieves mastery and status satisfaction in the current tier, transition to a broader map while carrying key character bonds forward.

#### 7.4 Story-Driven vs. Character-Driven Pacing & The 5-Chapter Cyclic Loop

- When the macro-plot is already dense with factions and twists, simplify events and prioritize **Character-Driven** scenes (desire clashes, contrast reveals, relationship shifts).
- When the cast is clear but the plot feels thin, prioritize **Story-Driven** escalation (new constraints, information asymmetry, ticking clocks).
- **5-Chapter Character Reaction Loop** (for stretching simple setups into high-engagement sequences through multi-party motives):
  1. *Beat 1*: Protagonist displays a capability -> triggers greed or ambition in Authority Figure A.
  2. *Beat 2*: Authority Figure A pressures Intermediary B -> Manipulator C covertly coaches B to exploit the situation.
  3. *Beat 3*: B approaches the protagonist -> C celebrates premature success in secret.
  4. *Beat 4*: B suffers a setback or emotional shift -> C feigns comfort while doubling down on the scheme.
  5. *Beat 5*: The payoff lands -> B realizes C's ulterior motive as the protagonist reaps the true reward.

#### 7.5 Immersion Maintenance & Genre Security Calibration

Maintain an unbroken emotional chain so the reader never pauses to critique plot contrivances with cold real-world logic:

- **Open with a "Save the Cat" micro-scene**: Showcase a relatable predicament (e.g., financial pressure, protecting an underdog) and a defining character trait in Chapter 1.
- **Layer three levels of empathy**: Surface empathy (relatable situation) -> Deep empathy (visceral emotion) -> Value identification (shared moral code).
- **Calibrate safety to genre**: In high-tension, survival, or crime/underworld genres, never give the protagonist an omnipotent patron who can erase consequences at will. Overwhelming safety destroys crisis tension.

### Step 8: Run the Quality Verification Checklist

Before finalizing any character sheet or scene audit, verify every applicable item:

- [ ] **Protagonist**: Emotional core motivation; fatal character flaw that causes plot mistakes; 3-5 visual anchors; signature hook; intact prestige; zero red-line violations (no preachy doormat/saint, mindless berserker, irredeemable sadist, stupidity-driven blunders, or self-destructive quitting).
- [ ] **Supporting Cast**: Explicit dramatic function; 1-2 sharp labels; planned exit; maximum 3 speaking supporting characters per scene; does not overshadow the protagonist.
- [ ] **Antagonist**: Matched to hierarchy tier; demonstrates competence on entry; internally logical motive; scores at least one real win (Mid-Boss+); ideological clash (Major Boss+); foreshadowed early (Final Boss).
- [ ] **Motivation Chain**: Specific inciting trigger; strong constraints preventing effortless steamrolling; believable failure risks.
- [ ] **Contrast & Consistency**: 3-layer label contrast (Identity / Performance / Core) with at least one inversion; minimum 3 strong plot ties; traits shown through action, sensitivity, and avoidance rather than narrator praise; stage-appropriate relationship behavior.

## Gotchas

- **Mistaking demographic tables for character design**: Listing height, birthday, and favorite food creates zero dramatic engine. Every attribute must either generate conflict, create contrast, or anchor reader memory.
- **Confusing "Moral Gratification" with "Saintly Doormat"**: Giving a protagonist a system or code that rewards righteous action works when it delivers status, power, and catharsis. Having a protagonist sacrifice their own interests out of naive mercy alienates readers.
- **Lowering villain IQ to force a win**: If the protagonist can only win because the antagonist suddenly forgets their resources or acts foolishly, both characters lose credibility. Upgrade the protagonist's tactical leverage instead.
- **Breaking immersion with info-dumps**: Never halt an emotional escalation to explain backstory or world rules. Reveal character history through present-tense choices under pressure.
